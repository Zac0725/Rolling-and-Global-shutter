3. The maths and meaning behind all this

The fan is genuinely rotating, but its entire image is not recorded at one instant. Each row sees the blades at a slightly different angle. That mixes different moments into one picture.

The twist: The fan did not bend. The camera recorded it at different heights row by row causing delay.

A camera contains a sensor which reads light, now, not all sensors read light the same you see. Some sensors read row by row whereas other sensors read the entire frame at once. There are three main variables which affect the final image knowing this information: revolution speed, full sensor readout times, and exposure per sensor row. A model we can use, is imagining a painter instead of a sensor. Full sensor readout is the amount of time taken for the first row to the last row to be read, so imagine if a painter is painting from top to bottom, but very slowly- an object moving in that painting will end up in a completely different position from when painting started! Exposure per sensor row is how long each row is exposed to light, a low amount of time per row ends up causing the image to have large black bars, this is because our normal LED light bulbs actually flicker! Though the naked eye can't see it, when exposure per sensor row ends up being reduced to extremely low numbers, the dark bars form from this flickering. Now, using our painter example, if we only lightly tap a brush super fast on the line then we'll end up getting choppy lines, but if we hold the brush for a while longer we end up getting a smooth, slightly blurry image, creating the 'motion blur' we see in household fans. Finally, when considering revolution speed we can imagine a fan pointing upwards at the start of 'painting', as we take time to reach the bottom, the fan has spun, and so now it’s pointing to the left, this creates an odd tilted shape to our image; This becomes much more noticeable as revolution speed increases.

The actual maths (and what the code computes)

For image row y in an image with H rows, its exposure begins at:

rowTime(y) = frameTime + direction × (y / (H − 1)) × readout

For bottom-to-top readout, direction means we use 1 − y/(H − 1) instead. The sample at a pixel is the average scene brightness across that row's exposure interval:

pixel(x,y) ≈ mean[ scene(x,y,rowTime(y) + exposureOffsetᵢ) ]

The blade angle at any instant is θ(t) = θ₀ + 2π × revolutionsPerSecond × t .

The simulation evaluates these equations in JavaScript. It computes the fan angle, fence position, or LED brightness for each readout strip and exposure sample.

Rolling VS Global Shutter

Rolling Shutter

A rolling shutter captures the image row by row, from bottom to top. This process happens in a quarter of a second thus normally does not affect the outcome of a photo or video. However, if something was to move faster than the shutter, say a propeller, the sensor will capture the propeller at a different position in every row making the image seem distorted. This limitation is mainly due to the fact that many cameras have a mechanical shutter that can only expose parts of the sensor at a time, as shown in the gif, furthermore, a rolling shutter approach takes less processing power as the processor only has to process one line at a time, thus generating less heat and making it cheaper.

Global Shutter

A global shutter solves the problems brought by rolling shutter. A Global shutter captures the entire image at the same time avoiding the problems brought about by capturing the image line by line entirely. However, to facilitate a global shutter a faster processor is required as it needs to process the entire image at once rather than a line at a time, and having a faster processor means more heat is generated. Due to the heat the camera size needs to be increased to fit fans to cool the processor, all of these extra factors make global shutter more expensive thus less common than rolling shutters
